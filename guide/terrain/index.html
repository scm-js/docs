Terrain

The Terrain layer's palette has six tabs. Five are brushes, and the sixth protects what they have laid:

Tab What it does
Isometric Paints terrain types and lays the cliffs and edges around them. The one to use for almost everything.
Rect Paints flat ground of one terrain type, with StarEdit's random variation. No cliffs, no edges.
Tile Lays any single tile, or a block of tiles picked from the tileset's sheet: cliff pieces, doodad pictures, anything the tileset has.
Blend Lays a tile next to one you picked, chosen from those whose facing edge continues it.
Heal Mends seams, the lines where two tiles' pictures do not meet, with look-alike tiles that play the same.
Pin Lays nothing. It marks tiles that the Isometric, Rect and Heal brushes and the fills then leave alone.

Drag to paint. [ and ] resize the brush from 1×1 to 7×7, Alt+click picks the terrain or tile under the cursor, and right-click offers Pick and Fill Area. The Properties panel breaks the tile under the cursor into group, slot, elevation, walkability and buildability.

Remove stranded units, at the top of the palette, deletes the ground units a stroke has just left where they cannot walk, as part of the same undo step. The game would remove them at the start. Buildings stay where they are: the game makes them on any ground.

The isometric brush#

The isometric brush is StarEdit's diamond brush. It sets the diamond under the cursor to a terrain and works outward from there: a neighbour that cannot border the new terrain becomes the terrain in between, and cliff faces stack as tall as the tileset draws them. A single click of high ground gives a small mesa; a wider brush gives flat high ground inside a cliff ring.

The brush works from the map's isometric record, a part of the file that only editors read. Protected maps often have it stripped; the Repair plugin rebuilds it as such a map opens, exactly where the ground was laid with the isometric brush and as a best guess elsewhere. The Isometric tab warns when the record and the ground disagree, and when part of the map is ground the brush cannot join up with, such as hand-placed tiles or another editor's work.

Rect, Tile and Blend#

  • Rect lays flat ground and nothing else, for filling an area you will shape afterwards, or for ground under a doodad.
  • Tile is for the pieces the isometric brush will not give you: a single cliff tile, a doodad's tile, a corner the cliff sets do not have. The buttons beside the filter show the tileset four ways: grouped rows (each tile group as a labelled block), one grid of every tile, the sheet and the pieces. The filter and the search box choose which groups any of the four lists. The tab comes back as you left it (view, filter and search) after a visit to another tab, until the editor is closed. The grid lists a tile once where the tileset holds the same picture with the same flags several times; the same picture where it walks, builds or stands at another height is another tile and is listed too.
  • Blend is for joins the cliff sets never had: dirt into a doodad's base, one edge set into another, a hand-laid shoreline. Click a tile on the map to make it the anchor, and the palette lists for each side the tiles whose opposite edge continues it, best seam first.

None of the three changes the isometric record, so the isometric brush does not know what they laid, and a later isometric stroke beside such tiles can lay over them. Pin the tiles you want kept.

The sheet and blocks#

The Tile tab's sheet view shows the tileset the way its artist drew it: one row for each tile group, sixteen tiles wide, the rows touching, with the group's number at the left. Pictures that run across tiles and groups show whole there: a doodad, a bridge, the buildings and walls a mod adds. A thin dashed line separates groups that are not consecutive. The button with two arrows beside the view buttons shows the tiles at full size, scrolling sideways when the palette is narrower.

A click picks one tile, as in the other views. A drag picks a block: a rectangle of tiles as wide and high as you dragged, which cannot cross a dashed line. With a block picked:

  • A click lays it. The Tile brush shows the whole block under the pointer, its top-left corner on the tile you point at, as a paste does. Empty places in the block leave the map as it is.
  • A drag repeats it. The press sets where the first copy goes, and every copy the drag passes over is laid next to the last, so a piece of wall or floor runs on without a seam. The whole drag is one step to undo.
  • The fills tile it. Fill Area and Fill Map with This Block repeat the block the same way, lined up on the tile you clicked or on the map's corner, and leave pinned tiles alone. With a block, Fill Area covers the same terrain around the tile you clicked, as the Rect fill does, not only the same exact tile.

While a block is picked the brush size shows the block's size. Picking a single tile again (a click in any view, the Tile # box, Alt+click on the map, or Single tile) goes back to the brush. A block belongs to the map and tileset it was picked on. Symmetry does not mirror a block: its pictures face one way, so it is laid under the pointer only, as a paste is.

Pieces#

The Tile tab's pieces view lists the parts of the sheet that maps lay whole: a wall, a ramp, a ruin, each drawn in its own outline. A click picks a piece as a block, to lay as above, and the sheet then shows where it came from. Hold the pointer over a piece for its size, its group and how often maps lay it; Most laid first puts the pieces maps use most at the top.

Nothing in a tileset says where one picture ends and the next begins, so the editor goes by how maps lay the tiles: two tiles that are neighbours in the sheet, and are laid as neighbours most of the times either is laid at all, belong to one piece. At first only the map in front is read. Learn from Maps… reads every map in a folder you choose and keeps what they lay in the browser for later sessions. When it is done, a notice says how many maps it read and skipped, and how many pieces each tileset now has, and the line above the pieces says how many learned maps this tileset's list comes from. Learning again replaces what was learned. Forget (the eraser beside the line) drops it, and so does Preferences ▸ Storage ▸ Clear all data.

  • The game's own maps give none. A map laid with the Isometric and Rect brushes lays no pieces, so the view is mostly for a mod's tilesets and for maps built tile by tile.
  • A repeated picture is one entry. Where a tileset holds one picture several times, exactly or redrawn with slightly different dots, the pointer says how many copies the entry stands for. A copy under other flags is listed right after it and says what differs.
  • Alt+click picks a whole piece. With pieces listed, Alt+click on the map in Tile mode picks the piece when the tile under the pointer is laid beside a neighbour from its piece, and the single tile otherwise.
  • What is learned belongs to the tileset's files. A mod read from its folder and the same mod installed share it, and a new version of a mod that changes a tileset starts that tileset afresh.
  • A mod or a plugin can name pieces. A mod's terrain file lists its pieces under the data set's name, and a plugin's named pieces come under its own heading; both stand ahead of the learned ones, and the search box finds them by name. Save as a terrain file… (the button with a down arrow) writes what the view lists into such a file, for a mod to ship (Terrain a mod declares).

Heal#

A seam is a line where two tiles' pictures do not meet: one tile ends lighter or darker than the next begins. Ground laid with the isometric brush has almost none; they show up around hand-placed tiles, stretched ramps and pasted blocks. While the Heal tab is open the map marks every seam with a pink line, as View ▸ Seam Overlay does, and the tab counts them.

Drag over a seam, and where the tileset has a look-alike tile that lines up better with its neighbours, Heal lays that tile instead.

Heal only takes a tile that is the same to the game: it walks, builds and blocks sight exactly as the old one does, at the same height, and is the same kind of ground. A stroke changes how the map looks and nothing about how it plays. Ground without a seam is left as it was laid.

The status bar says what each stroke did: how many tiles it changed and how many seams there were under it before and after. When it changed nothing, it says why:

  • No seam here. The ground already lines up.
  • The seam is on a placed doodad. Heal leaves a doodad whole. Convert it to terrain first if its tiles are what needs mending.
  • No look-alike tile. The tileset has no tile that lines up better and plays the same. Mend the seam by hand with the Tile or Blend brush.

The tab's button does the same over the marked area, or over the whole map when nothing is marked. On the game's own maps that removes about half of the seams and changes one tile in a hundred. A stretched ramp gains less, since few tiles play the same as a ramp's. Always show keeps the seams marked after you leave the tab.

Pin#

Pin keeps ground as you laid it. The isometric brush works out every tile around the diamonds it changes, so a stroke beside a ramp you built tile by tile takes part of the ramp with it. Drag the Pin brush over tiles to pin them, and such a stroke leaves them alone.

  • What leaves a pinned tile alone: the brushes that lay terrain by rule (Isometric, Rect and Heal), both fills and Replace Terrain. The status bar says when a stroke left pinned tiles alone.
  • What still writes it: whatever you point at the tile. The Tile brush, Blend, a paste and a doodad all lay over a pin.
  • The cliffs around it follow the stroke. The isometric brush still changes its own record under a pinned tile, so the ground around is drawn as if the tile had followed. Only the pinned picture stays.

The tab's Unpin choice turns the brush round. Pin All Hand-Laid Tiles pins every tile the map's isometric record does not account for, which on a map from another editor is the ramps, blends and stray tiles its maker set by hand. With an area marked on the Cut / Copy / Paste layer, it and the tab's other buttons work inside the area. A doodad placed as terrain is pinned as it goes down, unless Pin terrain doodads is unticked on the Doodads layer.

Pinned tiles carry a mark in their corner while the tab is open, and View ▸ Pinned Tiles keeps the marks on elsewhere. Pins are saved in the map file and move with their tiles when the map is resized. The game does not read them, and another editor will not keep them.

Overlays and tools#

Two overlays on the View menu show what the ground means to the game:

View ▸ Seam Overlay marks the seams, the places where the ground's picture breaks. It draws a line along every join between two tiles where the picture steps: one tile ends lighter or darker than the next begins, by more than either picture changes by itself at that edge. Rough rock hides a seam that a smooth slope shows, and the overlay weighs them the same way. The stronger the line, the plainer the seam. The Heal tab turns the overlay on while it is open.

Ground laid by the isometric brush is almost free of seams; a few stand around doodads and cliff faces, where the game's own pieces meet roughly. Hand-placed tiles, a stretched ramp and a pasted block show where they need touching up: Heal mends what it can without changing how the map plays, and the Tile and Blend brushes are for the rest. The overlay compares the classic pictures, so with Remastered Graphics on it marks the same seams, whether or not the 2x pictures hide them.

View ▸ Walkability (Ctrl+Shift+W) draws the ground as a unit walks it: islands, the areas the map divides into, the chokes between them with their widths, and the distances between start locations. In the editor at scmjs.dev its panel (Tools ▸ Walkability…) can also have the game itself walk the map: the regions the game plans routes over, real units walked between the start locations, a group of twelve units timed through every choke, and how fast each start location mines with 8, 16 and 24 workers.

File ▸ Import ▸ Terrain from Image… turns a picture into terrain with the isometric brush, over the whole map or a rectangle. Walkability and Terrain from Image are plugins, on from the start; see Plugins.

Tools ▸ Replace Terrain… turns every tile of one terrain type (or one exact tile) into another, over the whole map or the area marked on the Cut / Copy / Paste layer. Tools ▸ Fill Terrain lays the whole map anew. Both are one undo step.

Symmetry#

Symmetry mirrors what you do across the map's axes or about its centre. M or the toolbar button turns it on and off, and the list beside the button picks the mode: left ↔ right, top ↔ bottom, four-way, a half turn, quarter turns, or either diagonal. The last three need a square map. While a mode is on, the axes show on the map and the ghost shows where the copies will land.

  • The terrain brushes (all but Blend), the fills and the fog brush also paint on the mirror images.
  • Placing a unit, sprite, doodad or location places one at each image as well. Each copy goes to its own player: P1's start location gives P2's under the two-way modes, and P2's, P3's and P4's under the four-way ones. Neutral units and resources stay neutral. A doodad that would have to turn is skipped, and so is a copy that does not fit where it lands: on another doodad, or on the wrong ground.
  • Selecting something also selects whatever stands at its images. Dragging then moves them all as mirror images, resizing a location resizes its copies, and Delete removes them all. A map made symmetric by hand works the same way.

Each change is one undo step. Tools ▸ Symmetry… describes each mode and has the option to keep copies for the same player instead.

A doodad cannot be flipped, so across a left-right mirror its image is its twin: the doodad the tileset drew as its mirror image. A ramp for a cliff running down to the right gives the ramp for the cliff running up to the right, a bridge gives the bridge running the other way, and a stretched one gives its twin stretched the same. Every ramp and bridge of the game's eight tilesets has a twin, and selecting a doodad selects its twin too, so the two move together.

The status bar names the twin, and says when the game's two do not play alike: the two Desert cliff ramps differ in 83 small squares of height and ramp, the Ice ones in 16, and in Badlands, Jungle, Ashworld and Twilight the cliff ramps are exact images. Across a top-bottom mirror or a half turn a doodad has no twin, since no tileset has a ramp drawn facing up the screen, and the same doodad is tried there instead.

Is the map the same on every side?#

The Symmetry dialog also measures the map as it stands. Beside each mode is how much of the ground plays alike on every side under it, so a map opened from somewhere else shows which symmetry it was made with: a mirrored map reads 98% or more under its own mode and far less under the others, and a map drawn freehand reads low under all of them.

Under the mode you pick, The map as it is says how many small squares (a quarter of a tile each way, the size the game decides walking and height at) do not match their images, split into walking, height and building, and lists the largest places as buttons. A button applies the mode, goes to the place and turns on View ▸ Symmetry Differences, which paints each such square red, or amber where only building differs, on both sides. Mineral fields, geysers and start locations are checked too: each wants one of its kind at every image of its spot, holding the same amount.

Some differences are the game's, not yours. A tile cannot be flipped, so the far side of a mirrored map is built from other tiles, and the game's own ramp pairs do not carry quite the same flags: mirror a Desert cliff ramp and 69 small squares stand at another height on one side.
